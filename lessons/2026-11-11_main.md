## 2. Đọc rộng · truyện dài kỳ – Hope Street – Tập 1: The Key

Đây là tập đầu của **truyện dài kỳ** mình sẽ viết tiếp mỗi tuần. Đọc **một mạch** như đọc truyện, đừng dừng lại ở từ lạ. Những cụm <mark>tô vàng</mark> là chunk nên để ý.

> Nam arrived in Manchester on a wet Sunday in October. He had two suitcases, a backpack, and a piece of paper with an address on it: *Flat 3, 14 Hope Street*.
>
> The taxi stopped outside a tall brick building. It looked old, but the front door was painted bright blue, and there were flowers in the window next to it. Nam paid the driver, took his bags, and stood in the rain <mark>for a moment</mark>. This was it. His new home for the next two years.
>
> He had a new job at a design company in the city centre, and he was going to start on Tuesday. He was excited, **but** he was also nervous. He didn't know anyone here.
>
> The landlord, Mr Patel, had told him on the phone that the key was "under the plant pot by the door." Nam looked down. There were three plant pots. He checked the first one. Nothing. He checked the second one. Nothing again. He was lifting the third pot **when** the blue door opened.
>
> "Can I help you?" A woman in her sixties was looking at him. She was holding a cup of tea, and she didn't look very friendly.
>
> "Oh — hello. Sorry. I'm Nam. I'm <mark>moving into</mark> Flat 3. Mr Patel said the key was under a plant pot."
>
> The woman looked at him, then at his suitcases, then at the rain. Suddenly she smiled.
>
> "Under the plant pot? That man. He tells everyone that, and then he forgets to put it there. Come in, come in, you're <mark>soaking wet</mark>. I've got a spare key. I'm Margaret, Flat 1. I've lived here for thirty years, **so** <mark>if you need anything, just knock</mark>."
>
> Inside, the building was warm and smelled of toast. Margaret gave him the key and showed him the stairs. "Your flat's on the second floor. The heating's <mark>a bit</mark> old, **so** if it makes a strange noise, don't worry. It always does that."
>
> Nam carried his bags up the stairs. His flat was small: one bedroom, a tiny kitchen, and a living room with a big window. From the window he could see the whole street — the wet roofs, a small shop on the corner, and a bus going past.
>
> He sat down on the floor, **because** there was no sofa yet, and looked around. It wasn't much. **But** it was his.
>
> Then the heating made a loud *BANG*, and Nam laughed for the first time that day.

## 3. Đọc kỹ · khó hơn – Why we forget most of what we read

Bài này khó hơn một bậc. Đọc 1 lần lấy ý chính, sau đó đọc lại kỹ. Các từ nối và từ quy chiếu được **in đậm**.

> Most of us have had this experience: you finish a book you really enjoyed, and a month later you can <mark>barely</mark> remember what happened in it. It is not because your memory is bad. It is because of the way memory works.
>
> Psychologists have known for more than a century that we forget new information surprisingly quickly **unless** we use it again. In the 1880s, the German researcher Hermann Ebbinghaus tested himself on lists of <mark>nonsense syllables</mark> and found that much of what he had learned disappeared within a day. **This pattern, which** became known as the "forgetting curve," has been studied many times since.
>
> The good news is that the curve can be <mark>flattened</mark>. Each time we come back to an idea — by thinking about it, talking about it, or meeting it again in a different place — **it** becomes a little harder to forget. **This is why** reading the same kind of material, <mark>rather than</mark> jumping between completely different topics, helps language learners so much: the same words and phrases keep returning, and every return makes them stronger.
>
> <mark>In other words</mark>, remembering is less about how hard you try the first time and more about how often you meet something again.

| Từ / cụm | Nghĩa |

| nonsense syllables | âm tiết vô nghĩa (kiểu "zof", "dax") |
| flatten (the curve) | làm (đường cong) bớt dốc |
| rather than | thay vì |
| in other words | nói cách khác |

## 4. Nghe · khó – A short talk about sleep

Một người kể về giấc ngủ của mình, tốc độ tự nhiên hơn. Nghe 2 lần trước khi xem lời. Mục tiêu là **nắm ý chính**, không cần hiểu từng từ.

**Lời bài nghe (Claude đọc, tốc độ mặc định 1×):**

- **Speaker:** Can I tell you something about sleep that surprised me? For years, I thought I was just a bad sleeper. I'd go to bed around eleven, lie there scrolling through my phone, and somehow it would be one in the morning before I actually fell asleep. Then I'd drag myself out of bed at seven, feeling like I hadn't slept at all.

What finally changed things wasn't a special pillow or an expensive app. It was something embarrassingly simple: I started getting up at the same time every single day, weekends included. At first it was awful, honestly. But after about two weeks, my body started to get sleepy at roughly the same time each night, without me forcing it.

It turns out our bodies run on a kind of internal clock, and that clock cares far more about when we wake up than when we go to bed. So if you're struggling to fall asleep, don't start with the evening. Start with the morning.

## 5. Đọc · C1 – The jam study

Bài ở mức **C1**, câu dài và nhiều sắc thái. Hiểu được khoảng 50–60% đã là tốt. Cứ đọc và đoán, mục tiêu là tìm "trần" của Tân.

> In 2000, two psychologists published a study that would go on to shape a generation of marketing advice. Shoppers at an upmarket supermarket in California came across a tasting table offering either six varieties of jam or twenty-four. The larger display drew more attention — around sixty percent of passers-by stopped, compared with forty percent for the smaller one — **yet** only about three percent of those who stopped at the larger table went on to buy a jar, against roughly thirty percent at the smaller one.
>
> The finding was <mark>seized upon</mark> as proof that too much choice paralyses us, and the phrase "the paradox of choice" soon entered everyday conversation. What received far less attention was what happened next. When other researchers attempted to reproduce the effect, the results were strikingly inconsistent: in some settings, abundant choice discouraged decisions; in others, it made no measurable difference, or even helped. A 2010 analysis pooling dozens of such experiments concluded that, on average, the effect was close to zero.
>
> None of this means the original study was wrong. **Rather**, it suggests that whether choice overwhelms us depends heavily on circumstances — how well we understand our own preferences, how easy the options are to compare, and how much is at stake. The lesson, perhaps, is less about jam than about how readily a tidy story can <mark>outrun</mark> the evidence behind it.

## 6. Nghe · C1 – On being a beginner again

Một người kể chuyện học piano khi đã trưởng thành. Nói tự nhiên, nhiều thành ngữ. Nghe **2 lần** trước khi xem lời.

**Lời bài nghe (Claude đọc, tốc độ mặc định 1×):**

- **Speaker:** I took up the piano at thirty-four, which, if you've ever tried it, you'll know is a fairly humbling experience. There's something about sitting at a keyboard, fumbling through a piece a ten-year-old could play in their sleep, that strips away whatever sense of competence you've built up at work.

For the first few months, I'll be honest, I nearly packed it in more than once. What kept me going wasn't discipline, exactly. It was noticing that the frustration came in waves, and that each wave was followed, often out of nowhere, by a small breakthrough: a passage that had been impossible on Monday suddenly falling into place by Thursday.

I've come to think that being a beginner is a skill in itself, and one most adults let wither. We get good at a handful of things, and then we quietly arrange our lives so that we never have to look foolish again. Which is a shame, because the stretch where you're clumsy and slow is precisely where most of the learning happens.

So if there's something you've been putting off because you'd be terrible at it, well, yes, you probably would be. For a while. That's rather the point.
